01 · the thing you just felt

Cutting harder is easy, and mostly useless

Hire only facemen and watch what happens. The pile grows, the orb takes it back faster the bigger it gets, and your banked material stops moving while your output has never been higher.

Spoil on the floor is not yours. The orb reclaims it at a rate that rises exponentially with the size of the heap, so the binding constraint is never how hard you cut — it is whether you can move it before you lose it. That is the whole game in one sentence, and every system above it is a new way to bend that trade.

It matters because the game this replaces had exactly one bottleneck — money — for an entire career, which is why every upgrade in it was a rate and every decision had an arithmetic answer.

THE RECLAMATION RULE
loss/s = k · (spoil on floor) ^ 1.45

A bottleneck that moves, punishes hoarding, and cannot be solved by accumulating — only by throughput. Stolen wholesale from (the) Gnorp Apologue, whose rock reclaims shards from the second tier onward.

02 · the first six posts

Nobody on this list is a bigger number

Every post has to pass the same three-part test, taken from Gnorp's developer: it must change behaviour, it must stay optional, and it must work with the others. An upgrade every player must buy is a tax with a purchase button.

Faceman

Stands at the face and cuts. Steady, small, constant.

The baseline — and roster count is the multiplier, so power scales with the thing that is the game rather than with purchases.

Hauler

Never touches the orb. Moves spoil to the depot.

Produces nothing and is frequently the correct buy. The first time a player learns what the game is actually about.

Shotfirer

Places charges. Nothing, nothing, nothing — then a large collapse.

Burst instead of trickle. Out-cuts a faceman and is a net loss without haulage to clear the spike.

Borer

Cuts into the orb rather than at its face. Opens chambers.

Zero immediate yield. Chambers gate depth, depth gates the good material — the first buy that is an investment rather than a rate.

Rook

Works the heap itself, recovering what the orb is taking back.

Useless until you are failing. Counter-play to over-cutting rather than an upgrade to it.

Battery

A static installation. Fires on a cycle. Occupies no roster slot once sited.

The first automation — output that does not consume the scarce resource, which is slots and attention.

Read the interlock, because it is the design: shotfirers make haulers necessary; haulers make rooks unnecessary until you over-cut; borers pay nothing now and gate everything later; batteries buy back a slot, which is worth more than their output. And none of the six is mandatory — facemen and haulers alone will reach your first discharge.

03 · the ladder

Seven rungs, and each one is a different machine

A second prestige layer that is the same game with bigger multipliers buys a fortnight and costs more than it earns. So every rung has its own currency, its own upgrade mechanic and its own screen — a flat grid, then a tree with exclusive branches, then equippable rolls, then a compiler. Each reaches down into the rungs below and also feeds itself.

DUTY ROSTER Allocation. Who works where. Never goes away. one working
DISCHARGE A flat grid. One-off buys, nothing exclusive, always something affordable. It must never be able to trap a new player. a crew · hours
REASSIGNMENT An exclusive tree. Extraction, throughput or prospect — you cannot take all three, so postings diverge. This is where a build first exists. a district · days
COMMISSION Equippable rolled clauses. Rolled with modifiers, hold only a few, one form rerolls one clause. Already built — eighty of them, across six dimensions. a world · weeks
STATUTE A compiler. You write the standing instructions the department runs on: if spoil exceeds X, pull two facemen to haulage. a system · months
ENCLOSURE Placement. The dig needs more power than a world can make, so you fence a star and title its surface shell by shell. a star
THE AUTHORITY Allocation across offices — each running the whole ladder below. The mechanic from rung zero, six orders of magnitude up. The structure recurses, and a recursive structure has no top. many stars

The escalation is a chain, not a zoom.

One pair of hands only digs so fast. A seam runs out. Then — the load-bearing step — past a certain depth the rock is hot and dense, and digging stops being a labour problem and becomes a power problem. One world cannot generate enough to dig its own core. Quarrying the system for collectors is a rounding error against what the deep draw needs.

So you enclose a star. Not because the game ran out of ideas — because a deep enough hole needs a star to run it. The dig is always the point; the megastructure is infrastructure for it. You are still a case officer, and people are still going into a hole.

04 · why it does not end

Infinite games cannot be authored — only generated

No enumerated catalogue may exist anywhere. The game this replaces has a maximum depth of twelve, permits stopping at D-8, exactly nineteen unlocks and three tiers of everything. Those are lists, and a list ends — that is not a metaphor for why it runs out on day 39, it is the mechanism.

Every list becomes a function of the tier. Post n is a formula. Depth is unbounded. Clause rolls are generated rather than drawn from a fixed pool. It is a checkable rule: if a system is stored as an array of hand-written entries, it terminates.

And nothing here is drawn by hand, which is what makes generation possible at all — there is no artist on this project, so an orb, a world and an enclosure are the same renderer at three radii.

Decide before writing any code

The number type. A JavaScript double overflows to infinity a little past 1.8e308, and an infinite incremental goes there. It is exactly why Antimatter Dimensions' author wrote break_infinity.js.

It reaches further here than in a browser game, because the server is authoritative: every currency becomes a Decimal, Postgres columns become text with the arithmetic moved into the resolver, and the resolver must stay deterministic through all of it — the test suite asserts on replayed journals.

Retrofitting this is the migration that kills the project.

05 · the register

The paperwork never changes scale

You file the same form for a stellar enclosure that you filed for one recruit's boot allowance. That is the joke, and unlike most jokes it improves as the objects get more absurd — the engine behind both Universal Paperclips and Kittens Game.

The worshippers are a mechanic

An objection has been received regarding the disassembly of the orb. Pending review, the following are suspended: ordnance, shotfiring, and work on the fourth quadrant.

Challenges need a reason for the rule to change. A faction that objects, on principle, to the orb being taken apart is a restriction engine, a comedy engine, and free — and the same trick works at every scale. By the Enclosure rung the objection is to fencing a star, and the form is unchanged.

06 · where it stands

What is already built, and the one week that can kill it

The spine exists and it is good. Lazy resolution against a tick watermark — nothing runs per player between requests, and the server replays missed ticks in a transaction. Deterministic seeding on (character, tick). Storage behind a port with two adapters. Migrations that refuse to auto-run in production. A simulation harness that drives the real resolver over months of play.

Also already written: an automation ladder where every hire takes a standing instruction rather than a toggle, a challenge catalogue in the Antimatter Dimensions mould, and the eighty-clause system that turns out to be an entire prestige rung.

This is a spine transplant, not a rewrite. Roughly four to six thousand lines change.

Stage 0 · one week · headless

Six posts, a reclaiming pile, Decimal from the first line. No interface.

Run the allocation-spread test: do simulated players who allocate differently end the week materially apart?

If the answer is "always buy haulers", the lattice is decoration and everything above it is worthless. Stop there — it cost a week rather than a quarter.
